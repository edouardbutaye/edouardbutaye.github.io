Research

My work concerns the numerical simulation of turbulent and multiphase flows, and the resolution scales at which the physics of these flows can be described at an acceptable computational cost.

Hybrid RANS-LES simulations

Postdoctoral researcher · CEA Saclay · 2025 to present

In nuclear power plants, the cooling of the fuel assembly rods depends on a highly turbulent internal flow, with an associated Reynolds number of approximately $5\cdot10^{5}$ [1]. The operating conditions, a pressure of 150 bar and a temperature of 600 K, make experimental studies on the grids difficult. Numerical simulation is therefore used to characterise the flow.

Large eddy simulation gives access to the unsteady quantities of the flow and predicts accurately the force exerted by the fluid on the structure, but its cost prevents complete parametric studies. For one fuel grid configuration, a RANS computation required 40 million cells for a cost of 20 000 CPU hours, whereas the LES of the same configuration required 600 million cells for a cost of 6 million CPU hours [2]. An LES is therefore about 300 times more expensive than a RANS computation. Hybrid RANS/LES methods were developed to reduce that cost.

Implementation of the HTLES method in TrioCFD

The Hybrid Temporal Large Eddy Simulation (HTLES) formulation developed by Manceau [3] is derived from the Partially Integrated Transport Model of Chaouat and Schiestel [4]. Its interest lies in its mathematical foundation, in which LES is rewritten using a temporal filter. When the cutoff frequency approaches zero, the formulation approaches the RANS formulation, which is not the case for a conventional LES formulation based on a spatial filter. When the cutoff frequency approaches infinity, the formulation approaches Direct Numerical Simulation (DNS), and the modelled turbulent viscosity vanishes from the momentum equation. The transition is governed by the local ratio of modelled to total energy [5], rather than by a characteristic distance to the wall as in Detached Eddy Simulation [6].

The $k$-$\omega$ SST [7] HTLES formulation [5] was implemented in TrioCFD [8]. A turbulence forcing term was added to the momentum equation to address the modelled stress depletion (MSD) observed at the RANS/LES interface [9], also known as the grey area issue, which arises because the filter does not commute with the differential operators.

Identification of a physical remeshing criterion

In most hybrid RANS/LES computations, RANS is performed at the wall and LES elsewhere. Nevertheless, there may be regions of the domain where a RANS computation gives satisfactory results and does not require an unsteady simulation. In the backward-facing step configuration, a $k$-$\omega$ RANS simulation performs well upstream of the expansion, whereas the turbulence production is strongly underestimated in the detached shear layer downstream. In the general case, the physics is not known and these regions cannot be identified intuitively.

The definition of the mesh is one of the main issues of hybrid RANS/LES simulations. Two engineers studying the same industrial configuration will not obtain the same results, because they will not define the transition regions towards LES at exactly the same place. This raises difficulties for the certification of numerical results. The criterion sought must therefore be independent of the mesh and depend only on quantities accessible from a RANS computation.

Following David et al. [10], the ratio of production over dissipation computed from a previous RANS computation is used to locate the regions that would require an LES description. The grid is refined where

$$\frac{P_k}{\varepsilon} > \alpha_{\mathrm{th}}$$

$k$-$\omega$ SST computations were performed on the backward-facing step and on the periodic hill. The detached shear layer is well identified by the field in both configurations. The magnitude of the field appears to depend on the flow configuration, which motivates a sensitivity analysis on the threshold value.

For the refinement strategy itself, the target grid size is the one that reaches a prescribed ratio of modelled to total kinetic energy. The value of 0.2 was chosen, which amounts to resolving 80% of the turbulent kinetic energy [11]. That size is estimated by assuming a Kolmogorov spectrum [10].

Automated remeshing strategy

The procedure proposed by David et al. [10] requires several HTLES computations for the grid to converge, which is too cumbersome for industrial applications. The strategy is modified here to carry out a single remeshing step, relying on the RANS computation alone to identify the LES regions. The remeshing is performed with the Mmg remesher [12]. A sensitivity analysis on the threshold value of the ratio is carried out to assess its influence on the HTLES results. Even when the shear region is well localised by the criterion, estimating the required grid size from the RANS results can produce singularities. The grid size is therefore clipped to a minimum value, and a sensitivity analysis was carried out on that minimum as well.

The methodology targeted is the following. A mesh convergence study is first carried out with RANS computations. The physical criterion is then used to remesh the domain in the regions of interest. Finally, the hybrid RANS/LES simulation is run on the resulting mesh. The implementation of HTLES in TrioCFD was validated beforehand by comparison with numerical studies of the literature on the classical validation configurations of hybrid RANS/LES methods.

The finite volume-element method [13] used in TrioCFD requires tetrahedral meshes, and the $k$-$\omega$ SST model requires $y^{+}=1$ at the wall. A non-uniform, anisotropic tetrahedral mesh is therefore required in order to significantly reduce the computational cost.

Study configurations

Three configurations are considered: a periodic channel at $\mathrm{Re}_\tau = 590$ [14], a backward-facing step at $\mathrm{Re} = 5\,000$ [15] and a periodic hill at $\mathrm{Re} = 10\,595$ [16]. A grid convergence study was performed to ensure the convergence of the RANS fields.

Particle-resolved simulations of fluid-particle flows

PhD · PROMES – CNRS, University of Perpignan · 2021 to 2024

Solar tower power plants use the concentrated solar flux to heat a heat transfer fluid and generate electricity through a thermodynamic cycle. Increasing the thermal to electrical conversion efficiency requires raising the receiver outlet temperature to at least 800 °C. Fluidized particles are an alternative to conventional fluids, as they withstand temperatures in excess of 1000 °C without degradation of their physical properties and store heat efficiently. Characterising the flow within the receiver tube and the physical mechanisms of heat transfer requires a local description of the flow, which is obtained here with particle-resolved simulations carried out using high performance computing (HPC).

Particle-Resolved Direct Numerical Simulation (PR-DNS) is the finest scale of resolution and captures all the hydrodynamic interactions, but its computational cost does not allow the collective effects of several hundred thousand particles to be studied. Computational Fluid Dynamics - Discrete Element Method (CFD-DEM) simulations were developed for this purpose. In CFD-DEM the particles are smaller than the Eulerian cells, so the collective effects can be studied, but the force exerted by the fluid at the interfaces is not resolved and correlations are required to model it. These correlations have often been developed on academic configurations that are far from the configurations in which they are used. An intermediate scale of resolution is therefore proposed in this work.

Solver development in TRUST/TrioCFD

The collision detection method was optimised using the Link-Cell method associated with Verlet tables. The complexity is reduced from $N^{2}$ at every time step to $N$, and the overall computation time is reduced by 20%.

An original Lagrangian method was developed to compute the hydrodynamic forces exerted by the fluid on the particles and the heat flux received by the particles. The pressure and the velocity are interpolated trilinearly at two points $P_1$ and $P_2$ located at distances $\delta$ and $2\delta$ from the interface. The pressure is extrapolated linearly at the gravity center of the Lagrangian facets. The viscous term

$$\mu \left( \nabla \mathbf{u} + \nabla^{T} \mathbf{u} \right)\cdot \mathbf{n}$$

is computed at the gravity center of the facets following a second-order upwind scheme. The phase indicator is also computed at the faces and the edges of the control volumes, which improves the computation of the diffusive flux at the interface.

These developments were integrated into the TrioCFD software and are available to its other users. The simulations were carried out on several hundred processors on a national supercomputer, using the one-fluid formulation associated with the Front-Tracking method [18]. The most expensive simulation required up to 3 800 processors for about ten days of computation.

Hydrodynamics of a liquid–solid fluidized bed

Particle-resolved simulations of a liquid–solid fluidized bed of 2 134 particles, reproducing the experimental configuration of Corona [19], were performed. Four fluidization velocities were considered, corresponding to solid volume fractions ranging from 0.13 to 0.35, for grid resolutions of 12, 24 and 36 meshes per particle diameter.

The bed height and the solid fraction are well predicted even with the coarsest grid resolution, and the Richardson-Zaki law [20] is recovered. Higher spatial resolutions are required to capture the fluctuating kinetic energy profiles and the autocorrelation of the vertical fluid velocity. The radial distribution function of the particles shows the homogeneous nature of the bed. The particles tend to rise in the center of the bed and fall back near the wall.

The model of Alméras et al. [21] relates the fluid velocity variance to the solid fraction and to the mean fluid velocity. For a given solid fraction, the influence of the particles on the fluid agitation varies with their diameter and their density. A dependency on the terminal Stokes number is therefore introduced:

$$\langle u_{l,i}'^{\,2}\rangle_{(x,y,z)} = \zeta_i \, \frac{\langle U_{l,y}\rangle^{2}_{(x,y,z)}}{St_t} \, \alpha(1-\alpha)$$

The coefficients were computed using the least squares method, which yields $\zeta_v = 11.7$ and $\zeta_h = 4.82$. The correlation predicts the variance of the fluid velocity fluctuations within a 20% confidence interval. It was assessed on two additional configurations built from the settling velocity measurements of Mordant and Pinton [22]. The variance is well predicted for the first configuration. For the second one, at a terminal Stokes number of 10.6, the variance is underestimated by the correlation. Beyond a Stokes number of about 10, the wet restitution coefficient [23] begins to increase, and collisions between particles are expected to contribute to the energy transfer. This contribution is not accounted for in the model.

Heat transfer in an anisothermal fluidized bed

The same configuration was studied under anisothermal conditions for four fluidization velocities and three grid resolutions. The temperature field is extended inside the particles by a ghost-fluid method. The wall flux is evaluated to second order from the two fluid temperatures nearest the wall.

The macroscopic behaviour of the bed is well predicted with the coarsest grid. For a grid resolution of 12 meshes per particle diameter, however, the method fails to predict the thermal behaviour of the flow, and a grid resolution of 24 meshes per particle diameter is required to predict the heat transfer at a reasonable cost. The fluid Nusselt number increases with the solid fraction, in accordance with Gunn's correlation [24].

The wall-to-bed heat transfer coefficient increases with the solid fraction, in agreement with the correlation of Haid [25], which was established on nearly 2 500 experiments. The deviation from the correlation increases with the solid fraction, which is unexpected since, for the same grid resolution, the resolution of the flow decreases as the velocity increases.

The study of the velocity-temperature correlations shows that the wall-to-bed heat transfer is driven by the turbulent convective heat flux associated with the wall-normal velocity. The mean wall-normal velocity is almost zero, so the mean convective heat flux is small compared with the turbulent contribution. The velocity tangent to the wall does not contribute to the turbulent heat flow. The position of the agitation peak is strongly affected by the grid resolution, and moves from $x^{+} = 16$ for 12 meshes per particle diameter to $x^{+} = 27$ for 36 meshes per particle diameter.

Correlations of the particle Nusselt number from the literature were also assessed on 77 simulations of a settling particle in a quiescent fluid, for Reynolds numbers between 1 and 32 and Prandtl numbers between 0.1 and 10, at a resolution of 20 meshes per particle diameter. For two of the three correlations considered, the predicted heat flow is within 10% error.

Subgrid-corrected simulations (PR-SCS)

A distinction is proposed between PR-DNS, in which the velocity gradients and the pressure are fully resolved, and Particle Resolved Simulation (PRS), in which the grid resolution is of the order of ten meshes per particle diameter and the gradients are only partly resolved. The very high computational cost of the former limits its application to academic cases, which explains why simulations at a dozen meshes per particle diameter are commonly found in the literature. In these cases, the hydrodynamic forces exerted by the fluid at the particles' interface are under-resolved.

A mesh convergence study of the Stokes sedimentation of an isolated particle was performed for grid resolutions between 5 and 80 meshes per diameter. The convergence of the computed hydrodynamic force is of order 2. For a grid resolution of 10 meshes per diameter, a relative error of 10% is obtained on the force.

A mesh-dependent correction of the unresolved part of the friction and of the pressure was built from this convergence study. The resulting scale of resolution is named Particle Resolved - Subgrid Corrected Simulation (PR-SCS). The correction is validated for Reynolds numbers up to $10^{-1}$ and density ratios between 2 and 3000. A grid resolution of 10 meshes per diameter or higher is required to correctly capture the direction of the hydrodynamic force.

Gas-solid configurations were finally studied in order to validate the numerical tool for the simulation of these flows, which are the ones encountered in the solar receivers.

Multiscale simulations of two-phase boiling flows

Master's research internship · PROMES – CNRS · 2021

Direct steam generation is another route for concentrated solar power, in which the water is vaporised directly inside the receiver tube. In the Llo solar power plant, in France, Fresnel mirrors concentrate the incident solar radiation onto a horizontal tubular receiver. The water is injected at 70 bar, a few degrees below saturation, and a two-phase water and vapour mixture is obtained at the outlet of the lines, which can be up to 540 m long. The surface heat flux applied to the tube reaches 40 kW m$^{-2}$, to be compared with the 340 W m$^{-2}$ of the mean incident solar flux at the surface of the Earth.

The vapour volume fraction at the outlet, commonly called the void fraction, depends on the flow rate, on the injection temperature and on the surface heat flux. It is currently controlled by the plant operator and limited to 80%. Predicting the electrical production of the plant requires predicting this void fraction, and therefore characterising the flow within the tubes. The objective of this work was to validate a numerical simulation tool able to do so.

Euler–Euler modelling of a boiling flow in a horizontal tube

The NEPTUNE_CFD two-fluid model was used to study the behaviour of the refrigerant R141b in diabatic cases. The model is based on the Euler–Euler formalism of the Navier–Stokes equations, in which the governing equations are solved for both phases of the fluid at each time step. A mesh convergence study was carried out, which found that a grid resolution of 40 meshes per tube diameter was necessary for this configuration.

The simulation of horizontal boiling flows is a challenge. The succession of the flow regimes, namely bubbly, plug, slug, stratified and stratified wavy, has to be predicted. These regimes involve mass transfer between the phases and are characterised by multiscale physical phenomena. A method for the identification of the large interfaces is used. It detects the continuous phases, and also the presence of a dispersed phase within them, such as vapour bubbles in the liquid phase or droplets in the vapour.

Conjugate heat transfer with the tube wall

The conjugate heat transfer between the tube wall and the fluid was considered through a coupling with the SYRTHES 4.3 software, which solves the solid conduction in three dimensions. Traditional Neumann or Dirichlet boundary conditions do not represent a wall whose temperature responds to the flow it is heating. The thermal analysis of the configuration demonstrated the strong coupling between the temperature distribution in the solid and the two-phase flow regimes at stake in the fluid domain.

Validation and analysis of the flow

The approach was validated by comparison with an experimental study of the literature [27], based on the faithful reproduction of the positions of the two-phase flow regime transitions in the domain. The experiment consists of a refrigerant flowing in a horizontal serpentine tube heated by the Joule effect. The tube is transparent, which allows a qualitative comparison between the simulations and the experimental data, and results are reported for several inlet configurations.

The agreement was obtained through a parametric study of the physical models, namely the thermal forcing of the tube, the turbulence models, the surface tension and the parameters of the boiling flow, and of the numerical methods, namely the grid resolution, the interface sharpening, the time step and the number of nodes per CPU.

Original post-processing was used to unravel the flow characteristics. The mean and RMS fields of void fraction, temperature and streamwise velocity were analysed in several sections, once statistical convergence was reached. A thermal equilibrium was reached in the saturated liquid, but not in the vapour phase, due to the flow dynamics and possibly to the presence of droplets.
